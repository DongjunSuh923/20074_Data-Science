# Strategic Insights: Enhanced Trade and Domestic Broad Demand

This note records a non-final interpretation of the enhanced
- `Trade / external network shock`
- `Domestic broad demand shock`

It is intended as a working interpretation layer that can be extended after later cross-family comparison.

## 1. Enhanced Trade / External Network Shock

### 1.1 What changed relative to the first-pass trade shock

The first-pass trade track was mostly an `export + vessel exposure` stress test.

The enhanced version added:
- Census state imports
- combined total trade value
- partner exposure to `China`, `Canada`, and `Mexico`
- trade-war weighting using:
  - USTR Section 301 China actions
  - USTR Section 232 Canada/Mexico steel-aluminum episode

As a result, the scenario became less of a pure Gulf/Pacific export test and more of an
`import + manufacturing + container vulnerability` test.

### 1.2 Why the selected shock states changed

Selected states:
- `CA` severe
- `GA` medium
- `NJ` medium
- `MI` mild
- `IL` mild

#### `CA`
- California remained severe because it is still the largest overall trade gateway in the set and combines
  high total trade, high import intensity, and strong maritime/container dependence.
- In practical terms, this is the `Los Angeles / Long Beach` gateway effect:
  California matters not just because it exports, but because it absorbs and redistributes an enormous share
  of inbound container traffic.

#### `GA`
- Georgia rose because the enhanced version rewards states that are highly exposed to import and container flows.
- This is consistent with Georgia's official freight framing around the Port of Savannah and the state's logistics role.
- Georgia therefore appears less as an export commodity state and more as a `Southeast container distribution state`.

#### `NJ`
- New Jersey rose for similar reasons, but with an East Coast profile:
  large container-port dependence, dense warehousing/distribution, and access to one of the largest consumer markets in the country.
- In other words, New Jersey is not just a port state; it is a `port + inland warehouse + Northeast demand` state.

#### `MI`
- Michigan rose because the enhanced scenario now captures partner-country dependence much better.
- Michigan's trade is deeply tied to `Canada` and `Mexico`, especially through manufacturing and automotive supply chains.
- This means Michigan behaves less like a coastal trade state and more like a `cross-border industrial trade state`.

#### `IL`
- Illinois rose because it is a major inland intermodal and manufacturing state.
- Once imports, multimodal distribution, and trade-war partner exposure are considered together, Illinois becomes a
  strong `interior trade redistribution hub` rather than just a domestic freight state.

### 1.3 Why the key support candidates survived

Non-must-have next 15:
- `NJ, WA, NE, AL, OR, SC, GA, MN, WI, NV, NC, KS, VA, UT, CO`

These candidates can be grouped into three realistic roles.

#### A. Coastal / container reinforcement
- `NJ`, `WA`, `OR`, `GA`, `SC`
- These states either are direct port/container gateways or are tightly linked to them.
- They survive because the scenario tests what happens when major trade gateways are stressed but not removed from the national system.

#### B. East Coast inland connector reinforcement
- `NC`, `VA`
- These two states repeatedly appear because they can support coastal trade flows while also serving inland domestic demand corridors.
- `NC` is more of a `Southeast growth and distribution reinforcement` candidate.
- `VA` is more of a `port-backed Mid-Atlantic corridor reliability` candidate.

#### C. Interior redistribution / fallback support
- `NE`, `MN`, `WI`, `KS`, `CO`, `UT`, `NV`
- These states survive not because they are primary ocean gateways, but because they can absorb or re-route inland freight
  when trade-heavy coastal states are stressed.

### 1.4 Why the overlap with NGL looks the way it does

Enhanced trade next-15 vs NGL footprint:
- overlap: `WA, NE, AL, OR, MN, WI, NV, KS, CO`
- scenario-only: `NJ, SC, GA, NC, VA, UT`
- NGL-only in the next-15 comparison:
  - `AZ, CA, CT, FL, IA, IL, IN, KY, LA, MA, MI, MO, MS, NY, OH, OK, PA, RI, SD, TN, TX`

Important caveat:
- this is a `next-15 excluding must-have` comparison.
- Therefore, some states that appear as `NGL-only` are **not** unimportant in the trade scenario.
- For example:
  - `CA` is the severe shock state
  - `IL` and `MI` are selected shock states
- They show up as `NGL-only` only because the comparison list excludes core must-have states and focuses on expansion/support candidates.

So the trade gap should be read as:
- `scenario-only` states are places where trade resilience logic suggests extra reinforcement beyond the current NGL footprint
- not as a claim that all `NGL-only` states are unnecessary

### 1.5 Practical interpretation

The enhanced trade track suggests three meaningful reinforcement directions:
- `NJ` and `GA`: container-heavy East/Southeast gateway reinforcement
- `NC` and `VA`: East Coast inland connector reinforcement
- `UT` and parts of the interior set: secondary inland fallback options

Among these, the strongest strategic signal is that
`NJ / GA / NC / VA`
are not simply large freight states; they are the states that become more valuable once trade vulnerability is defined as
`imports + containerization + partner-country dependence`, rather than exports alone.

## 2. Domestic Broad Demand Shock

### 2.1 What this shock is actually testing

This track is not a route-closure or corridor-failure shock.

It is a `broad consumer-demand slowdown` test built from:
- BEA real PCE
- BEA real GDP
- total employment
- recent growth of those series

So the key question is:
- if large consumer and demand-heavy states soften, which non-core states still remain attractive as support candidates?

### 2.2 Why the selected shock states make sense

Selected states:
- `FL` severe
- `TX` medium
- `CA` medium
- `NY` mild
- `WA` mild

#### `FL`
- Florida rose to severe because it combines very large consumer spending with strong recent growth.
- In real terms, Florida is a population-growth and consumption-growth state with large freight demand pull.
- A broad domestic slowdown there matters because it affects inbound distribution, retail replenishment, and statewide consumption.

#### `TX`
- Texas combines scale and growth.
- It is not only a production state but also a huge consumption and distribution state.
- That makes it a natural medium demand-shock state even outside commodity-specific energy logic.

#### `CA`
- California remains essential because it is still the single largest consumer and freight market in the country.
- Even without a route-capacity shock, a broad demand slowdown there affects national distribution priorities.

#### `NY`
- New York appears because of its extremely dense consumer market and its role in Northeast demand concentration.
- It is less about statewide manufacturing and more about `demand density and downstream distribution`.

#### `WA`
- Washington appears as a milder version of the same story:
  a meaningful consumer market plus multimodal freight dependence.
- It behaves as a smaller but still important Pacific demand and distribution state.

### 2.3 Why the support candidates survived

Non-must-have next 15:
- `WA, NE, LA, AL, OR, MN, WI, KS, CO, OK, SD, VA, NV, NM, NC`

This support set has a different meaning from the trade track.

#### A. Regional redistribution support
- `WA`, `LA`, `OR`
- These states combine regional gateway functions with enough internal distribution mass to keep showing up after core-demand states are shocked.

#### B. Plains / interior balancing support
- `NE`, `MN`, `WI`, `KS`, `CO`, `OK`, `SD`, `NM`, `NV`
- These states survive because they help rebalance freight away from the very largest demand centers.
- They are less about coastal gateway exposure and more about `internal network continuity under softer demand conditions`.

#### C. East / Southeast reinforcement
- `VA`, `NC`
- These two remain visible because domestic demand stress still leaves value in the East Coast and Southeast growth corridor.
- `NC` reflects the southeastern growth belt.
- `VA` reflects Mid-Atlantic corridor and distribution support.

### 2.4 Why the overlap with NGL looks the way it does

Domestic broad-demand next-15 vs NGL footprint:
- overlap: `WA, NE, LA, AL, OR, MN, WI, KS, CO, OK, SD, NV`
- scenario-only: `VA, NM, NC`
- NGL-only in the next-15 comparison:
  - `AZ, CA, CT, FL, IA, IL, IN, KY, MA, MI, MO, MS, NY, OH, PA, RI, TN, TX`

Again, the key caveat is the same:
- this is a `next-15 excluding must-have` comparison
- so `FL`, `TX`, `CA`, and `NY` appear in the `NGL-only` side only because they are already core or selected-shock states,
  not because the scenario says they are irrelevant

The meaningful new signal is the `scenario-only` set:
- `VA`
- `NM`
- `NC`

Among those, `NC` and `VA` are the strongest because they also appeared repeatedly in the earlier domestic tracks
(`construction / infrastructure` and `bottleneck / congestion`).

### 2.5 Practical interpretation

This broader domestic demand track is weaker than the construction-material and bottleneck tracks, but it still adds useful structure:
- it confirms that `NC` and `VA` are not just bottleneck-specific anomalies
- it shows that even a softer, broader demand slowdown still preserves value in East/Southeast reinforcement states
- it also supports a more general role for interior balancing states such as `NE`, `KS`, `MN`, and `CO`

## 3. Consolidated takeaway from the enhancement pass

The enhancement pass changes the scenario analysis in two important ways.

### Trade
- The trade story is no longer `ports + exports only`
- It is now closer to:
  - `ports + imports + containers + partner-country risk`
- That is why the candidate set shifts toward `NJ`, `GA`, `NC`, `VA`, and `UT`

### Domestic demand
- The domestic story is no longer only:
  - `construction/material demand`
  - `congestion/reliability stress`
- It now also includes a softer `broad consumer-demand slowdown`
- That is why `NC` and `VA` gain additional support as recurring reinforcement candidates, while `NE`, `KS`, `MN`, and `CO`
  remain strong interior balancing candidates

## 4. Working strategic message

If the project needs a concise working message at this stage, it is:

- `NC` and `VA` keep surviving because they are not tied to just one mechanism.
  They matter in domestic demand, domestic congestion, and trade-connected inland distribution.
- `NJ` and `GA` become more important once trade shock is defined with imports and container dependence rather than exports alone.
- `CA`, `FL`, `TX`, `NY`, `IL`, and `MI` remain important, but many of them are already core or selected-shock states,
  so a simple `next-15 vs NGL` comparison can understate their scenario relevance.
