# Strategic Insights: NC and VA Under Domestic-Issue Shocks

This is a working interpretation note, not a final conclusion. It records why `NC` and `VA` repeatedly appeared as key reinforcement candidates under domestic-issue simulations.

## 1. Why this note matters

Under domestic-issue simulations, `NC` and `VA` repeatedly survived as non-core reinforcement candidates even though they were not fixed as the original `must-have` core set.

This matters because:
- the result is not random repetition across scenarios;
- both States appeared under **different domestic mechanisms**;
- this makes them stronger candidates than one-off scenario-specific states.

## 2. Simulation fact pattern

### Construction / infrastructure domestic shock

`NC` and `VA` both appeared in the construction/infrastructure hybrid candidate set.

Interpretation:
- this track represents **domestic demand weakening** in material-linked sectors;
- states that survive here are plausible receivers or stabilizers of demand reallocation.

### Domestic bottleneck / congestion shock

`NC` and `VA` both also appeared in the domestic bottleneck candidate set.

Interpretation:
- this track represents **interstate reliability/congestion stress**;
- states that survive here are plausible corridor substitutes, buffers, or alternative support states when reliability declines elsewhere.

## 3. Why `NC` plausibly survives in both domestic tracks

Official North Carolina planning materials suggest a freight structure that helps explain the result.

### Growth and demand side

NCDOT's `NC Moves 2050` states that North Carolina expects major population growth, with much of that growth concentrating along the `I-85 corridor` from Charlotte to Durham. It also notes this growth can increase congestion and reduce reliability.  
Source: [NC Moves 2050](https://connect.ncdot.gov/projects/planning/nc-moves-2050-plan/Pages/default.aspx)

Interpretation:
- under construction/infrastructure shocks, `NC` is not just a pass-through state;
- it is also part of a growing southeastern demand belt that can absorb redistributed domestic activity.

### Freight corridor side

North Carolina freight plans emphasize the importance of pass-through freight on `I-95`, `I-85`, and `I-40`, and explicitly frame the State as a place that could capture freight currently moving through neighboring port systems.  
Source: [Statewide Logistics Plan for North Carolina](https://www.ncdot.gov/initiatives-policies/Transportation/freight/Documents/StatewideLogisticsPlan_080513.pdf)

NC Ports also highlights access to `I-40`, `I-95`, `I-85`, and `I-77`, plus Wilmington/Morehead City port access and the Charlotte inland port.  
Source: [NC Ports Fast Facts](https://ncports.com/about-the-ports/fast-facts/)

Interpretation:
- under bottleneck shocks, `NC` has the structure of a southeastern corridor-support and redirection state;
- it can matter both as a destination-growth state and as an alternative routing/support state.

### Practical reading

`NC` looks like a **growth-and-distribution reinforcement state**:
- strong enough demand base to matter when domestic material demand shifts;
- strong enough corridor position to matter when East Coast reliability degrades.

## 4. Why `VA` plausibly survives in both domestic tracks

Official Virginia freight materials suggest a different but equally strong explanation.

### Port and multimodal access

Virginia freight planning documents emphasize:
- strong access to ports and major airports;
- access to large Eastern Seaboard and Midwest markets;
- the Port of Virginia's role as a major deepwater East Coast gateway.  
Source: [Virginia Freight Plan / Freight Element](https://www.transportation.gov/mission/office-secretary/office-policy/freight/freight-infrastructure-and-policy/virginia-freight)

Interpretation:
- under domestic demand shocks, `VA` is not only a consumer-region support state;
- it also functions as a distribution and intermodal gateway state.

### Corridor and reliability structure

Virginia's freight network includes major interstate freight spines such as `I-95`, `I-81`, `I-64`, `I-85`, and `I-77`.  
Source: [Virginia Multimodal Freight Network Tables](https://www.transportation.gov/freight/MFNTables/Virginia)

Regional freight planning in Hampton Roads also notes that the Port of Virginia is a major freight asset but that tunnel approaches can create freight bottlenecks.  
Source: [Hampton Roads Freight](https://www.hrtpo.org/402/Freight)

Interpretation:
- `VA` is exposed to bottlenecks because it is important;
- under reliability shocks, it is exactly the type of state that appears as a strategic reinforcement candidate because it sits at the intersection of port access and major interstate freight corridors.

### Practical reading

`VA` looks like a **corridor-and-port resilience reinforcement state**:
- it matters when domestic demand remains concentrated on the East Coast;
- it matters even more when congestion and reliability degradation affect East Coast freight movement.

## 5. Why both `NC` and `VA` matter more than one-off candidates

The strongest point is not that `NC` or `VA` ranked first in every list.

The stronger point is that both States:
- survive under a **domestic demand deterioration** mechanism; and
- survive under a **domestic reliability/congestion** mechanism.

That makes them more compelling than one-off scenario states because they are not tied to only one type of shock logic.

## 6. Working strategic interpretation

At this stage, the best reading is:

- `NC` is a **southeastern growth and distribution reinforcement candidate**;
- `VA` is a **Mid-Atlantic corridor and port resilience reinforcement candidate**.

This does **not** automatically mean NGL must expand into both States immediately.

It does mean that, under domestic-issue simulations, these two States repeatedly emerge as the most credible reinforcement candidates because they combine:
- demand-side relevance,
- corridor relevance,
- and East Coast network relevance.

## 7. Not-final caution

This note should remain provisional.

It is an interpretation layered on top of:
- state-level simulation outputs,
- current candidate ranking logic,
- and official freight planning documents.

It should be updated if:
- commodity-specific expansion logic is added,
- portfolio economics are incorporated,
- or additional scenario families shift the overlap pattern.
